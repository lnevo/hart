# Neville Island coke, cement, and chemical history

**Prototype reference for HART** · September 2026

The east-end Neville Island industrial complex began with an American Steel & Wire blast furnace, became Davison Coke & Iron’s coke, iron, and cement works, and operated for decades as Pittsburgh Coke & Chemical. The later names still used on HART — Shenango, Calgon, Kosmos, and Aristech — are the pieces of that one plant.

---

## How the plant worked

The east end of Neville Island was one industrial machine.

**Coke and iron.** Bituminous coal is baked in by-product ovens into metallurgical coke. The coke, plus iron ore and limestone flux, goes into a blast furnace to make pig iron. The ovens also make a rich chemical off-gas. That coupling lasted on Neville Island into the late 1980s. Pig iron stopped around 1990–91; coke then ran as a merchant product for other steelmakers’ blast furnaces until January 2016.

**Cement from slag.** Molten blast-furnace slag is the leftover calcium-silicate glass from ironmaking. Ground and used as a pozzolan, it is a cement ingredient. National Bureau of Standards testing identified Green Bag portland-pozzolan as a Pittsburgh Coke & Chemical product “in which the pozzolanic agent is a blast furnace slag.” Bureau of Mines yearbooks describe the Neville Island works making mostly slag portland cement by the wet process, with limestone barged down the Monongahela from a Green Bag mine near Morgantown. Davison built the cement plant next to the old American Steel & Wire furnace in 1929 because the iron works supplied slag and the river supplied stone.

**Chemicals from coke-oven gas and tar.** A by-product coke plant recovers tar, ammonia, and light oils (benzene, toluene, xylene). Naphthalene from tar is the classic feedstock for phthalic anhydride, used in plasticizers and alkyd resins. That chemistry put a 1956 Lummus phthalic plant and a 1959 maleic-anhydride unit on a coke-and-iron property, produced PC&C’s coal-tar epoxy coating patents, and later appears in USS Chemicals / Aristech records as phenolic resins, maleic anhydride, and a “Tar Ben Plant.” Activated carbon is the other coal product: in 1942 the government asked Pittsburgh Coke & Chemical to replace coconut-shell carbon in military gas masks with bituminous-coal granular carbon — the line that became Calgon.

**Coal in, coke and iron out; slag to cement; tar and light oil to chemicals; coal to carbon.**

## Overview

Around 1900, American Steel & Wire bought former farmland on the southern and eastern end of Neville Island, built a blast furnace, and built its own industrial railroad, the Pittsburgh & Ohio Valley. In 1929 Davison Coke & Iron bought that furnace property and built new coke ovens and a cement plant. Davison was reorganized out of bankruptcy in 1936 as **Pittsburgh Coke & Iron Company**. After chemicals were added around 1940, the firm took the name **Pittsburgh Coke & Chemical** (PC&C), commonly dated 1942.

During the 1960s PC&C broke apart:

## 1. American Steel & Wire (c. 1899–1929)

Neville Island was still known as Pittsburgh’s “market basket” when wire and steel capital arrived. In late 1899 American Steel & Wire announced a furnace plant on the island. Contemporary newspapers described a large project — six 800-ton Bessemer furnaces, 400 acres, and 5,000 jobs. The plant that was built was a blast furnace on about **182 acres** of former farmland on the southern tip, purchased in 1900.

The **Pittsburgh & Ohio Valley Railway** was incorporated 5 December 1899, wholly owned by American Steel & Wire, built February 1900–October 1902 on AS&W land, and interchanged with the **PC&Y** on the island. A 1923 Pittsburgh Plan note lists P&OV as a U.S. Steel industrial line of 2.04 miles, also connecting toward the P&LE.

In 1901 AS&W itself was folded into the new United States Steel Corporation, so the Neville Island furnace sat inside the USS system well before Davison arrived. During World War I the Ordnance Department commandeered P&OV (20 September 1918–1 July 1919) for a planned island ordnance plant.

By 1916 other firms on the island were making pig iron, scrapping steel, refining oil, and making concrete. The AS&W furnace site is the physical ancestor of the later coke, cement, and chemical complex.

## 2. Davison Coke & Iron (1929–1936)

Davison Coke & Iron Company was incorporated in 1928. In 1929 it bought the old American Steel & Wire blast furnace and built **new coke ovens and a cement plant** on the property.

The Tax Court and Third Circuit later described the company in the mid-1930s as a Pennsylvania corporation with a by-product coke plant and blast furnace on Neville Island, buying coal principally from W.J. Rainey, Inc. and Hillman Coal & Coke. The Hillman interest remains in this story through PC&C, the later park donation, and Calgon’s ancestry.

The Depression hit hard. A 1932 out-of-court plan failed. In 1935 Davison filed under Bankruptcy Act §77B. Final decree 31 January 1936. The reorganized company issued new common stock and debentures and **amended its charter to the name Pittsburgh Coke & Iron Company**.

---

## 3. Pittsburgh Coke & Iron, then Pittsburgh Coke & Chemical

Pittsburgh Coke & Iron is the company name from 1936 into the early 1940s. A 1940 annual report still uses it. Chemical products were added in 1940. University of Pittsburgh cataloging and Calgon’s corporate history treat **Pittsburgh Coke & Chemical** as the wartime identity, from about 1942.

By the 1950s PC&C was a diversified coal-chemicals company:

- **Phthalic anhydride** (1956 Lummus plant) for plasticizers and alkyd resins.
- **Maleic anhydride** (1959 authorization, 20 million lb/year) at the main plant.
- **Coal-tar epoxy protective coatings** (U.S. patents assigned to PC&C and to Pittsburgh Chemical Company).
- **Granular activated carbon** from bituminous coal (1942 military; 1955 “Pittsburgh Pulse Bed” sugar decolorization; 1960 drinking-water treatment).
- **Pesticides**, Agricultural Chemicals Division, 1949–1955.

Hillman capital remains in the background (J. Hartwell Hillman, Jr. / Henry L. Hillman). After the operating businesses were sold, residual land on the west end went to Neville Land Company, was donated for a park in 1977, and became the Ohio River Park Superfund site after the county found benzene, toluene, and chlorinated phenols in 1979.

## 4. Coke and iron → Shenango

Shenango was founded in 1962 when **Shenango Furnace purchased Pittsburgh Coke and Chemical’s coke-and-iron works**. Coke ovens and chemical operations on that site dated from about 1930. In July 1951 PC&C registered a rights offering of 140,243 common shares (one new share for each four held, not underwritten, Chemical Bank and Trust as subscription agent) to pay for construction that would raise pig-iron output from the Neville Island blast furnaces from **300,000 to 600,000 net tons a year** — a second furnace, in service about 1952. A 1963 *Commercial and Financial Chronicle* note says the prior year’s PC&C figures still included the former Coke and Iron Division, by then part of Shenango Inc., a 50% unconsolidated subsidiary.

Shenango kept the blast furnaces. Pig iron was still a Neville Island product in the late 1980s. On **13 June 1988**, when Shenango pleaded guilty to Clean Water Act discharges, EPA described the plant as producing **coke and pig iron** “using a blast furnace, coke ovens, and other ancillary equipment.” Molten iron also fed Shenango’s separate **ingot-mold foundry** farther west on the island (3100 Grand Avenue) — a 1939 Shenango-Penn Mold purchase, not the 1962 PC&C coke-and-iron deal. A plant veteran dates that foundry shutdown to **January 1990**; Toxics Release Inventory reports for the foundry division drop from tens of thousands of pounds in 1989 to 5 pounds in 1990.

The furnaces themselves came off around the same time. There is no company filing that names the last cast. What is on record is the close of the iron department and the departure of the pig machine. Don Marion, general foreman of the blast-furnace, steam-and-power, and pig-machine departments, dates his Neville Island iron work through March 1991 and writes that after the iron department closed, National Material bought the pig-casting machine, he dismantled it, and he rebuilt it at Bethlehem Steel’s Sparrows Point as Maryland Pig Services, where he was plant manager from July 1991. Plant veterans say the stacks were taken off in stages, one furnace first while the other kept running. A 5 December 1990 *Pittsburgh Post-Gazette* mill-closure map is described, by someone who looked at that page, as accompanying an article on shuttering a Neville Island blast furnace; that page has not been transcribed here. After the iron department closed, the east-end plant was a **merchant coke works** only. EPA’s later site writeup still mentions the 1952 second furnace in the history paragraph, but describes current operations as one **56-oven, four-meter coke battery**. A 2008 EPA iron-and-steel greenhouse-gas inventory lists Shenango at Neville Island among coke plants, not among operating blast furnaces.

Those Neville Island stacks are not the Shenango Furnace blast furnaces at **Sharpsville**, Pennsylvania. The Sharpsville No. 3 furnace was banked in 1968 and torn down in the late 1970s; No. 1 came down in 1970.

Coke outlasted iron by about 25 years. DTE Energy bought Shenango in 2008. Citing the North American steel downturn, DTE announced closure on 17 December 2015. **The last coke came off on 6 January 2016** — that is the coke date, not pig iron. (2014–15 news about Shenango is pollution enforcement against the coke battery.) Demolition included the May 2018 stack implosion. Lindy Paving closed on the site in November 2020.

This is the branch HART cards as Shenango Coke Works.

## 5. Cement → Green Bag, Marquette, Lone Star, Kosmos

Green Bag cement used blast-furnace slag as its pozzolan, with limestone barged from a Green Bag mine near Morgantown. After Marquette took over, Bureau of Mines yearbooks still describe mostly slag portland cement by the wet process.

In late 1960 and January 1961, PC&C agreed to sell wholly owned Green Bag Cement Co. to Marquette Cement Manufacturing Co. so PC&C could concentrate on other businesses. The FTC later recorded the 1961 acquisition and a Neville Island plant capacity of 1.5 million barrels, serving western Pennsylvania, northern West Virginia, and northeastern Ohio. EPA’s national cement survey lists Marquette at Neville Island, wet process, 1.75 million barrels.

Marquette became a Gulf & Western subsidiary in 1976 and was sold to **Lone Star Industries** in 1982. Pennsylvania PUC records show truck shipments originating at **Lone Star Cement Company, Neville Island** in 1987.

By the mid-1990s **Kosmos Cement Company** (Southdown 75%, Lone Star 25%) operated cement plants in Kosmosdale, Kentucky, and Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. A 1996 Allegheny County / EPA order names Kosmos as owner/operator of Portland cement production at 200 Neville Road. Eagle Materials bought the Kosmos business in 2020, including the Pittsburgh terminal at that address. The Kosmos site today is a **cement terminal** (bulk Type IL, masonry, packaged portland), not a kiln plant.

## 6. Activated carbon → Calgon

When the United States entered World War II, military gas-mask filters used granular activated carbon made from coconut shells. Faced with a shortage, the government asked Pittsburgh Coke & Chemical to make a substitute from a domestic raw material. In 1942 PC&C produced coal-based granular activated carbon from bituminous coal. Heat “activates” the coal by opening its pore structure; one pound of the finished carbon has on the order of a hundred acres of internal surface, which adsorbs organic chemicals from liquids and gases.

After the war the Activated Carbon Division stayed on the island:

- **1955** — the “Pittsburgh Pulse Bed,” the first activated-carbon system for sugar decolorization.
- **1960** — granular activated carbon pioneered for drinking-water treatment.
- **1962** — 40,000 pounds of bituminous-coal GAC installed for Virginia-American Water Co., a benchmark municipal installation.

The business was organized as Pittsburgh Activated Carbon Company, a PC&C subsidiary, and **Calgon Corporation acquired it in 1965**. Calgon itself was older and separate: it began as the Hagan Corporation (1918), whose chemist Ralph E. Hall developed the water-conditioning chemical sold as Calgon. After 1965 the carbon plant kept its own manufacturing and marketing. Merck bought Calgon in 1968. In 1985 management bought the carbon business out of Merck and formed **Calgon Carbon Corporation** (IPO 1987; NYSE 1991). **Kuraray** completed its acquisition of Calgon Carbon on 9 March 2018.

The Neville Island plant sits on the southeast corner of the island, east of the Fleming Park Bridge (EPA lists 200 Neville Road). EPA’s 2001 site visit described three operations still on the property: **carbon reactivation**, **impregnation and drying**, and **manufacture of activated carbon in pellet form**. Calgon still lists Neville Island among its U.S. reactivation sites. In August 2003 EPA determined that no further RCRA corrective action was required at the plant. Products from the island feed drinking-water and wastewater treatment, industrial purification, odor and acid-gas control (impregnated pellets), and the company’s reactivation service — spent carbon comes in by rail or truck, is restored, and goes back out.

## 7. Industrial chemicals → USS Chemicals → Aristech → Sunoco → BASF / Ashland

Phthalic and maleic anhydride, phenolic-type chemistry, and coal-tar coatings are documented on Neville Island in the PC&C era. EPA later lists USS Chemicals, Neville Island, among phenolic-resin producers and records maleic-anhydride operations at the same plant.

Pennsylvania Human Relations Commission (*Worthy v. Aristech*) records that United States Steel formed **USS Chemicals Division in 1966** to produce and market chemical products, and that the Neville Island facility was included. The activated-carbon business went to Calgon in 1965; the industrial-chemical plant went to U.S. Steel.

In 1986 USX transferred substantially all USS Chemicals assets to newly formed **Aristech Chemical Corporation**. Neville Island is in that transfer. Aristech’s SEC filings describe two businesses: **chemicals** (phenol, acetone, bisphenol-A, aniline, phthalic anhydride, 2-ethylhexanol, and plasticizers) and **polymers** (polypropylene and acrylic sheet). Those polymer plants were elsewhere. Polypropylene was made at Neal, West Virginia, and LaPorte, Texas, for fibers, carpets, diapers, automotive parts, packaging film, and injection-molded goods. Acrylic sheet was made at Florence, Kentucky (bathtubs, spas, glazing, signs, countertops). What Neville Island made in the Aristech years was **plasticizers**.

Aristech’s 1998 10-K rates Neville Island at **210 million pounds per year of plasticizers** for **flexible vinyl (PVC)**. Plasticizers are organic esters, made in a batch process on the Ohio River plant from phthalic anhydride and alcohols such as 2-ethylhexanol (those intermediates were produced at Pasadena, Texas). Finished plasticizer went to the merchant market, shipped mainly by **railcar or truck**. Downstream PVC uses listed in the filing:

- consumer goods — rainwear, toys, boots
- housing — flooring and wall coverings
- automotive — seat and dashboard covers, vinyl roofs
- industrial — wire and cable insulation

EPA generator lists also distinguish “Aristech Chemical Corp. – Neville Island” and “Aristech Chemical Corp. – Tar Ben Plant.”

In **April 1995** Aristech sold its unsaturated polyester resin (UPR) and maleic-anhydride business to **Ashland**. Aristech kept the land and the plasticizer units; Ashland took the UPR/MA equipment under an easement and a shared-services agreement. From 1995 on, two chemical names already shared the island: Aristech plasticizers and Ashland polyester.

A Mitsubishi-backed management buyout took Aristech private in 1990 (about $27/share, about $877 million; tender completed 7 March 1990). On **1 January 2001**, Sunoco bought all outstanding Aristech stock from Mitsubishi for **$695 million**. Neville Island is named as one of five plants in the deal. Company-wide capacity in the filing was 1.5 billion lb/year polypropylene, over 1.6 billion lb/year phenol and derivatives, and 800 million lb/year plasticizers — the polypropylene and phenol figures are other plants; the island’s contribution was plasticizers.

After Sunoco:

- **January 2004:** BASF buys Sunoco’s phthalate plasticizer *business* and the Pasadena, TX plant. Neville Island remains with Sunoco and toll-manufactures plasticizers for BASF, plus 15-year terminalling.
- **By 31 March 2007:** plasticizer manufacturing at Neville Island stops.
- **December 2007:** Ashland purchases the remaining facility and uses it as a terminal for BASF product shipping, bordered east and northeast by Shenango.

On HART the plant is named **Aristech Plastics**, the name it carried from 1986 through 2000. The prototype island plant made plasticizer for flexible PVC, not polypropylene pellets.

## 8. Pesticides and the west-end dump

PC&C made pesticides from 1949 to 1955. From 1952 into the 1960s, coking sludges, cement wastes, and pesticide wastes went into trenches on the west end of the island. ExplorePAHistory ties the shift from river dumping and burning to burial with the federal Water Pollution Control Act of 1948 and Pittsburgh smoke-control politics. There was no operating successor. The Superfund remedy is a separate land-use story from the east-end plants crews switch.

## 10. On HART

The island industries crews switch are the surviving trade names of this east-end complex:

- **Shenango Coke Works** — the coke-and-iron remainder. Pig iron ended about 1990–91; coke ran through 6 January 2016.
- **Calgon Carbon** — the 1942 coal-carbon line, under the Calgon name from 1965.
- **Kosmos Cement** — the slag-cement works, later a river terminal at 200 Neville Road.
- **Aristech Plastics** — the phthalic, plasticizer, and resin line during the Aristech years (1986–2000).

The island’s industrial railroad ancestor is American Steel & Wire’s **Pittsburgh & Ohio Valley**, interchanging with the **PC&Y** (later Pittsburgh Industrial / Ohio Central / **POHC**).
