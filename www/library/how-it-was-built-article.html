Bringing the Railroad Together

How AI grew up alongside the railroad, from the first research to the way the software, research and website are organised today.

This railroad started in 2022 as a tabletop layout, and there was very little AI in that part of the story. Over the next four years it grew into a full layout, a research archive, a set of fictional stories, an operating system with cards and paperwork, and the website you are reading.

AI took part in most of that, and the tools changed as the work did: first ChatGPT, then Cursor, and now Claude. This is the journey in the order it happened.

01A tabletop and very little AI

The first layout sat on a table in 2022, three years before the room that holds the railroad now. The gallery still has a photo of it, dated August 2022.

That is the honest starting point. It was a tabletop layout, and there was no AI to speak of. Everything that follows grew from there, and the order matters, because the way I use AI changed as the railroad asked for different things.

02Research and the first AI

AI came in through the research. It started in early 2025 with ChatGPT, which was part writer and part research partner. First came the roster of equipment on hand, and the early questions about what to model and in which era. That ran from about March into April.

Then I had to choose which real corridor to interpret and write the fictional backstory that goes with it. That took the spring and ran into summer. The identity work followed, and the logo is AI-generated. So are the fictional short stories, which were drafted with AI. ChatGPT also generated just about all of the document files, which is a lot of paper for one hobbyist.

Meanwhile the building had begun. The Build page has progress photos from February 2025 on. The track plan was settled in July. The industry research ran on through the second half of the year, and it is the base of the Prototype pages. Later planning, benchwork and helix design conversations went through ChatGPT too.

At this stage AI was a tool that wrote documents. That was fine for documents, but a layout also needs software.

03Code enters

By February 2026 the work had moved from documents to software. I began using Cursor, an AI code editor, and used it to edit the LCOS project, the software behind the layout electronics1. That repository's first commit is from February 2026.

Cursor then helped with the layout-editor work that came out of AnyRail, later with CATS, and with the rest. The difference from chat was plain. The AI was now working in the same files I was, so the result was something I could run, not only a document to read.

04Paperwork and operating

By then the planning was giving way to paperwork. A railroad that runs needs cards, and cards need pictures. AI helped match photos to the roster for the car cards.

In summer 2026 the traffic system, STS, came next, with an AI coding assistant helping tune its simulation. The project history credits Cursor from July. The operations story tells that part, and I wrote it up for The Cannon Ball2. This page only places it in the order of things: by now the AI was helping with the running of the railroad as well as the writing about it.

05Too many places

So by the summer of 2026 the project was real and busy, but it was scattered: documents and research in their own folders, the fiction in its drafts, control software for the layout in one project, the traffic simulation in another, car records, the bridge to the electronics, and the website. Each worked on its own, and nothing made sure they agreed. A change to a car name in one place might never reach the cards, the website or the simulation.

That was the problem I set out to fix in August.

06One place

The place I chose is a repository, a folder where every change is recorded, so anyone can see what changed and go back. This one began on August 7, 2026. By the end of the month the traffic software, the car data and the website had been gathered in, with Cursor doing that August work.

Claude took over the consolidation from mid-October, as I began testing outside Cursor, and the folders were tidied in October. One habit came with the move: important choices are written down as short decision records, each saying what we decided, why, and what it replaced. A tool that forgets everything between sessions can still read them. That is the quiet change in how I use AI. Early on I asked it for documents. Now I give it a place to work and a set of written rules to follow, and I ask the same of every tool that comes after.

07Where it arrived

On the software side, the layout data, the panels and the signals now sit together as plain files, with one naming rule so switches, signals and tracks are called the same thing everywhere. Signals run on the stock behaviour of JMRI3; How the signals work has the reasoning, and Electronics & the bridge covers the link to the electronics. Traffic and car records are kept the same way, so a car has one name from the card to the simulation. Some work stands on its own as pinned public projects inside this one: LCOS_ESP32_MQTT_Client1, sts-docker4, sts-docker-helpers5 and model-railroad-skills6.

The research is kept together, with a note of where each source came from. Every item in the historic archive carries its credit or source. A research helper looks things up and returns proposals with their sources, and I decide what goes in. No source, no caption.

The website has sections for different readers, and its pages are drawn from one source, so a change is made once, in one place. A page-building helper makes the pages. A reviewer helper reads the site as a first-time reader, a historian or a designer would, and this article was shaped that way.

Behind all of it, every tool reads one set of written rules.

Chat is a scratchpad; decisions live in the wiki.

The reason is plain. An agent knows only what is written down. A few helpers each have one job, and small automatic checks catch mistakes before they spread. One of those checks says of itself that it is "a guardrail against mistakes, not a security boundary", and I take that literally. A skill is a written guide the AI loads when a topic comes up, and an MCP is a plug-in tool it can call. My public plugin, model-railroad-skills, shares six skills and two read-only tools with other modelers.

08What stays with a person

Today it all lives in one place: the software that runs the layout, the research behind it, and the pages you are reading. A few helpers each do one job, and they all read the same set of written rules.

I run the railroad, and I decide what goes out and what is true. The AI does not run the railroad. What grew up beside it is a way of working: write it down, give each helper one job, and keep the judgment with the person who knows the layout.
