In January 2025 this railroad was only an idea, and AI came into it very shortly after. At first that meant ChatGPT, helping me research a place and write it down. Nothing about it looked like a way of building a railroad. It grew one job at a time, as the layout kept asking for something new.

Less than two years later, that help has drafted the documents and the stories, written the software that reaches the layout's electronics, planned a helix and produced the printed parts that built it, and helped pull the whole project into order. This page follows that journey as it happened: what I was trying to build, which tool I used, and what each step left behind.

01An idea, and the first AI

The current layout became an idea in January 2025, and AI came in very shortly after, through the research. ChatGPT was part writer and part research partner. First came the roster of equipment on hand, and the early questions about what to model and in which era. That ran from about March into April.

Then I had to choose which real corridor to interpret and write the fictional backstory that goes with it. That took the spring and ran into summer. The identity work followed, and the logo is AI-generated. So are the fictional short stories, which were drafted with AI. ChatGPT also generated just about all of the document files, which is a lot of paper for one hobbyist.

Meanwhile the building had begun. The Build page has progress photos from February 2025 on. The track plan was settled in July. The industry research ran on through the second half of the year, and it is the base of the Prototype pages. Later planning and benchwork conversations went through ChatGPT too.

For the first months AI was a tool that wrote documents. That was fine for documents, but a layout also needs software, and the first of that arrived in August.

02Code enters

The first code came in August 2025. traincode was an ESP32 client that connected to JMRI over MQTT, the first project where I started playing with those pieces together, and all of it was done with Cursor, an AI code editor. It became the foundation of the current client, though we later switched to a Nano bridge for LCOS compatibility.

The difference from chat was plain. The AI was now working in the same files I was, so the result was something I could run, not only a document to read.

03The bridge

From about October 2025 the electronics took over. I chose LCOS hardware for the field bus, and Cursor helped me plan and build a bridge between LCOS and JMRI1. It began as an ESP32 idea built on traincode, and by early 2026 it had become a Nano bridge running over a serial link. The work continued through April 2026.

Electronics & the bridge has the longer account.

04The wiring

A railroad that is going to be wired has to know what it is wiring. In November and December 2025 I had a hand-drawn ASCII sketch of the layout, made of plain typed characters. With Cursor it became a spreadsheet of switches, signals and locations, and then a versioned wiring inventory workbook.

That workbook later fed the wiring documents. Around the same time I tried some early experiments with JMRI Operations Pro, which did not become the car system.

05From track plan to panels

With the list of what existed, the next job was getting the track into JMRI. The plan had been drawn in AnyRail. Between about January and June 2026, Cursor wrote a pipeline that turned AnyRail exports into JMRI Layout Editor panels, the schematic drawings JMRI uses to know where the track, turnouts and blocks are.

Each generation of that pipeline did the job better than the one before, and the first production panel went live in May 2026. Cursor helped with CATS, the dispatching software, along the way too.

In March 2026 I also made a side project for other modelers, STL Studio, a block editor for writing RR-CirKits Tower LCC+Q logic. The railroad does not use it.

06Cards and the traffic simulator

By then the planning was giving way to paperwork. A railroad that runs needs cards, and cards need pictures. AI helped match photos to the roster for the car cards.

In June and July 2026 the traffic system, STS, came next, with Cursor helping to extend it and tune its simulation. The operations story tells that part, and I wrote it up for The Cannon Ball2. This page only places it in the order of things: by now the AI was helping with the operating side of the railroad as well as the writing about it.

07The desks

August 2026 was the busiest month. The CATS CTC desk was drawn in Designer as the dispatcher's desk, and the stock JMRI CTC machine was built beside it as an alternate. The Dispatcher System was set up to run trains automatically, and Signal Mast Logic ended up owning the aspects. Grok also helped for part of the conversion of route logic into native Signal Mast Logic.

08Too many places

So by August 2026 the project was real and busy, but it was scattered: documents and research in their own folders, the fiction in its drafts, control software for the layout in one project, the traffic simulation in another, car records, the bridge to the electronics, and the website. Each worked on its own, and nothing made sure they agreed. A change to a car name in one place might never reach the cards, the website or the simulation.

That was the problem I set out to fix.

09One place

The place I chose is a repository, a folder where every change is recorded, so anyone can see what changed and go back. This one began in August 2026. From the end of August the traffic software, the car data and the website were gathered in, with Cursor doing that work. I took stock of what lived where, and the operator portal, which had run only from a local server, moved to a domain of its own.

Claude took over the consolidation as I began testing outside Cursor. The folders were tidied, and the generic knowledge went into a public plugin for other modelers. One habit came with the move: important choices are written down as short decision records, each saying what we decided, why, and what it replaced. A tool that forgets everything between sessions can still read them. That is the quiet change in how I use AI. Early on I asked it for documents. Now I give it a place to work and a set of written rules to follow, and I ask the same of every tool that comes after.

10The helix

While the files were coming together, the biggest physical job on the railroad was under way: the helix that climbs from staging to the main level. The planning for it was some of the most valuable AI work on the whole project. Radius, grade, clearance, how the roadbed would be cut and joined, and the order it would all go up were worked through in conversation with ChatGPT before any wood was cut. All of that became a construction plan and a written design record.

Then the work moved to Claude, to build the files and carry the effort on, and it left the screen. The plan became scripts, the scripts became STL files, and the STL files became printed parts: router templates, height spacers, shims and other fixtures, 47 files in all. These were not models of anything. They were tools, and they went on the bench and into the build. The frame went up, the roadbed followed, and in about a month the helix was finished and tracked.

That was a real milestone. Until then the AI's work had been documents and software. Now it was something I could pick up and use to build the railroad. The helix page has the plan and every fixture, and The Build has the photos.

11Where it arrived

On the software side, the layout data, the panels and the signals now sit together as plain files, with one naming rule so switches, signals and tracks are called the same thing everywhere. Several signal systems were tried through September before I settled on the stock behaviour of JMRI3; How the signals work has the reasoning, and Electronics & the bridge covers the link to the electronics. Traffic and car records are kept the same way, so a car has one name from the card to the simulation. Some work stands on its own as pinned public projects inside this one: LCOS_ESP32_MQTT_Client1, sts-docker4, sts-docker-helpers5 and model-railroad-skills6.

The research is kept together, with a note of where each source came from. Every item in the historic archive carries its credit or source. A research helper looks things up and returns proposals with their sources. No source, no caption.

The website has sections for different readers, and its pages are drawn from one source, so a change is made once, in one place. A page-building helper makes the pages. A reviewer helper reads the site as a first-time reader, a historian or a designer would, and this article was shaped that way.

Behind all of it, every tool reads one set of written rules.

Chat is a scratchpad; decisions live in the wiki.

The reason is plain. An agent knows only what is written down. A few helpers each have one job, and small automatic checks catch mistakes before they spread. One of those checks says of itself that it is "a guardrail against mistakes, not a security boundary", and I take that literally.

12Tools you can reuse

Some of what came out of this is not about this railroad at all. Along the way the AI learned how JMRI, CATS and STS really behave, and that knowledge is worth sharing, so I packaged it for anyone to use on their own layout.

model-railroad-skills is a public plugin for Claude Code. A skill is a written guide the AI loads when a topic comes up, so it works from the right notes instead of guessing. There are six: editing JMRI panel files safely, signal mast logic, the Dispatcher System, JMRI and MQTT, running CATS on JMRI, and running STS in Docker. An MCP is a plug-in tool the AI can call, and there are two, both read-only, so the AI can look at what JMRI and the layout are reporting. Every rule was checked against the software's own source, and nothing in the plugin is specific to this railroad.

13More ways to use it

Looking back along that timeline, what stands out is not any one tool. I still use all three. Claude is taking over from Cursor, and ChatGPT still has its own usefulness. What changed is how much I ask of them: first documents, then software, then parts I could hold, then a whole project in order.

Each new use made the next one easier to see, and the value keeps growing as we find more ways to use it. The main level comes next, and I expect it will find a few more.
