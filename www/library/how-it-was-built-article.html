Bringing the Railroad Together

The journey from a tabletop layout to a working railroad, how AI helped along the way, and how the software, research and website are organised now.

The railroad grew from a tabletop layout in 2022 into a full layout, a research archive, a set of stories and a public website, and AI took part in most of it. This page follows that journey in the order it happened, then describes how the pieces are organised today.

The railroad's traffic, the cards and the session paperwork, has its own account in the operations story. This one is about everything around it: how the railroad came together, what AI did along the way, and why I now trust the files I work from.

01Where it started

It began with a tabletop layout in 2022, three years before the room that holds the railroad now. There was very little AI in that part of the story.

The larger layout took shape from there. The Build page has progress photos from February 2025 on, and the research that grew up around the building is where AI comes in.

02Research, stories and the first AI

The research started in early 2025, and about April is the first solid reference I can point to. ChatGPT generated just about all of the document files. All of the fictional short stories were drafted by AI. The logo is AI-generated. The track plan was settled in July 2025.

Documents, drafts and sources were piling up by then, and the layout needed software to match, which is where the next stage began.

03Software arrives

I used ChatGPT for a long time, until I began using Cursor, an AI code editor, to edit the LCOS project, the software behind the layout electronics1. That repository's first commit is from February 2026. Cursor then helped with the layout-editor work that came out of AnyRail, later with CATS, and with the rest. The panel layouts that JMRI3 displays were part of the same build-out.

The operations work is one more place AI helped. It matched photos to the roster for the car cards, and it ran the heavy iteration when I tuned the traffic simulation. The operations story tells that part, and I also wrote it up for The Cannon Ball in Summer 20262.

04Everything in different places

So by the summer of 2026 the project was real and busy, but it was scattered: documents and research in their own folders, the fiction in its drafts, control software for the layout in one project, the traffic simulation in another, car records, the bridge to the electronics, and the website. Each worked on its own, and nothing made sure they agreed. A change to a car name in one place might never reach the cards, the website or the simulation.

That was the problem I set out to fix in August, and the picture below shows both sides of it.

05Gathering it into one place

The place I chose is a repository (repo for short), a folder where every change is recorded. A commit is one saved snapshot with a note saying why. A branch is a side copy of the whole folder where work can happen without disturbing the main line; when it is good, it is merged back. Anyone can see what changed, when, and go back.

This repository's first commit was on August 7, 2026. By August 31 the traffic software, the car data and the website had been gathered in, and Cursor is credited in git for that August work. Claude then took over the consolidation, as I began testing outside Cursor, from mid-October, and the top-level layout of folders was tidied in October.

Two ideas ran through it. Each important choice is written down as an ADR, an architecture decision record: a short page saying what we decided, why, and what it replaced. And there is one real copy of each thing, so there is never a question of which version to trust. For the layout that means one tables file, one panel save and one car repository, each named in its own ADR. For the documents it means one folder, and a website that holds no copies of them.

These counts describe this repository only, as of October 31: about 680 commits on 47 days, at least 367 credited to Cursor and at least 270 to Claude. The helpers and automatic checks I describe below arrived at the very end of October.

06How it is organised now

Three things now share one set of files: the software that runs the layout, the research behind it, and the website you are reading. Each has its own habits.

Software

The layout data and the control panels are kept as files, with one tables file that describes the layout to JMRI. Because they are plain files in one place, a script can check them, and a change to a panel shows up in the history like any other edit. Signals run on JMRI's stock behaviour rather than a cleverer set; How the signals work has the reasoning. The bridge links JMRI to the signal and turnout electronics; Electronics & the bridge covers its role. The traffic simulation and the car records have their place in the operations story.

One public naming grammar for equipment (ADR-005) keeps switches, signals and tracks called the same thing everywhere. One rule I keep: repair the data, not the package. If a stock JMRI tool fails on this layout, I fix our files and leave JMRI alone.

Some of the work belongs to projects that stand on their own. A submodule is a pinned snapshot of another repo, kept inside this one, so nothing shifts underneath it unnoticed. The public ones are LCOS_ESP32_MQTT_Client1, sts-docker4, sts-docker-helpers5 and model-railroad-skills6. Cursor and Claude edit this code. I set the direction, and what runs on the layout is my call.

Research

The sources are kept together, each with a note of where it came from. On the site, every item in the historic archive carries its credit or source, and what cannot be credited stays out. The same research feeds the Prototype articles.

A research helper looks things up and returns proposals with their sources. I decide what goes in. A proposal with no source does not become a caption, and a caption with no source does not go on the site. That keeps the history on this site something a reader can check.

Web design

The site has sections for different readers, as the About page says. The Prototype is for anyone curious about the island, Operations for the people who come to run trains, Behind the Scenes for how the layout works, and The Build for the construction.

Pages read their text from data files, so a caption is changed in one place. Published documents come from one source through a list, so the site holds no copies of them. Only files that are checked in are published, and a build check catches broken links or private paths before anything goes out. A page-building helper may edit only the website folder, and a reviewer helper reads the site through a named lens, such as a first-time reader, a historian or a designer. This article was itself shaped that way.

07Working with AI

Having several tools means keeping them to the same rules. All of mine live in one file, and every tool I use reads it. It holds the commands, the list of things never to do, and a close-out routine for any task that changed files: update the wiki, commit on a branch, push, test, deploy where something live changed, then report what was done.

Chat is a scratchpad; decisions live in the wiki.

That line is near the top of the file, and the reason is plain. An agent starts each session knowing only what is written down, so anything not written down is lost. The wiki has a live status page that says what is true today, and a separate dated log that says what happened and when.

Some jobs go to a subagent, a helper with its own written instructions and one bounded task. I have five. Four only read: one traces why a signal or panel behaves as it does, one audits the repository for dead code and stale notes, one reviews the website through a named lens, and one researches history and sources. The fifth builds website pages and may edit only the website folder. None of them commits or publishes. The main session does that, and checks the result. A helper with one job is easier to trust than one that does everything, and when its answer is wrong, it is easy to see why.

Everything crosses the same steps: a branch, a pull request, the checks, a merge. Small automatic scripts called hooks run just before an agent executes a command and can say no. They exist to stop an agent doing what it should not and to ask me before anything that could affect the layout. One of them says of itself that it is "a guardrail against mistakes, not a security boundary", and I take that literally. Every pull request also runs a set of automated checks on the tables, the signals and the agent instructions themselves.

Two more terms, once. A skill is a written guide the AI loads when a topic comes up, so it works from the right notes instead of guessing. An MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a plug-in that gives the AI a tool it can call. My public plugin, model-railroad-skills6, has six skills and two read-only tools. Every rule in it was checked against the software's own source. The two tools have been tested against stand-ins, not a live layout, and neither can change the railroad.

08What stays with a person

I run the layout, I decide what goes out, and I decide what is true. The AI reads, drafts, traces and builds pages and files; it does not run the railroad.

It began as a tabletop in 2022. It is now a layout with its research, its stories, its software and its public view in step.
