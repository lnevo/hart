    The big picture

    Every train on a main track needs authority to be there. The exam covers the ways railroads have given that authority:

    • Timetable and train orders (TT&TO), from the mid-1800s into the 1980s. The timetable authorizes scheduled trains; the dispatcher changes things with written train orders delivered by operators. The exam's rulebook for this is the 1949 Standard Code.
    • Track warrants (TWC) and Direct Traffic Control (DTC): the dispatcher gives authority directly by radio, with no operators. The rulebook is the GCOR.
    • Signals: block signals, interlockings and CTC, where the signal itself gives the authority.

    On top of these sit general rules that apply everywhere: hand and whistle signals, flag protection, restricted speed and yard limits.

    1. The words

    The Standard Code gives each of these words an exact meaning, and the rules depend on it.

    • A regular train has a schedule in the timetable. An extra train does not; it runs only on a train order. Extras come in three kinds: plain Extra, Passenger Extra and Work Extra.
    • The time-table is the whole book: the authority for regular trains plus special instructions. A schedule is one train's line in it, giving its class, direction, number and movement.
    • When one schedule needs more than one train, they run as sections (First 7, Second 7). Every section except the last carries signals (green) to warn others another section follows.
    • Main track runs through yards and between stations and is used by timetable, train order or block signals. A siding is auxiliary to it, for meeting and passing. Single track carries trains both ways.
    • A schedule shows at most two times at a station. If it shows two, they are arrival and departure. If it shows one, read it as the leaving time.
    • Block words: a block is a stretch of track governed by block or cab signals; a block signal stands at its entrance; an approach signal warns of the signal ahead; an automatic block system is a run of blocks the trains themselves control.

    The GCOR adds modern terms: control points, controlled (absolute) signals, distant signals marked D, spring and variable switches, current of traffic, clearance point. Read the glossary cards for these.

    2. Who goes first

    Under TT&TO there is no dispatcher watching each move. Crews work it out themselves from superiority, which comes in three kinds:

    • Class, set by timetable: first class beats second, second beats third.
    • Direction, set by timetable: between trains of the same class only, the timetable names one direction superior.
    • Right, set by train order: the dispatcher can give any train right over another, and right beats both class and direction.

    Extras are inferior to every regular train. On double track (the D- rules) direction drops out, since opposing trains are on separate tracks; superiority is by right or class only. The S- rules are the single-track versions.

    Schedules expire. A regular train that falls more than twelve hours behind its schedule at a station loses its right and its schedule, and can then move only on train orders.

    Before leaving its starting station, a junction, or the end of double track, a train must find out whether every superior train that is due has arrived or left, usually from the train register. It must also have a proper signal to start, and it may never leave a station before its schedule's leaving time.

    Keeping out of the way

    Example: eastbound No 7 (first class) due to leave Cedar at 9:40 and Dale, the next station east, at 10:05.

    • A slower train ahead of No 7, going the same way, must be in the clear when No 7 is due to leave the station behind it. If the slow train is between Cedar and Dale, it must be clear by 9:40, not 10:05.
    • An opposing extra must clear No 7's time with a safety margin. The Standard Code margin is 5 minutes; the TT&TO primer quotes 10 (see Guide notes). An extra waiting at Dale for No 7 must be clear by 10:00 under the Standard Code. If an inferior train can't clear in time, it must flag (Rule 99).
    • At a meeting point the inferior train takes the siding. Two extras meeting: the one going the inferior timetable direction takes it. Two trains of the same class with a scheduled meet: the inferior one only has to clear the main before the superior train's leaving time, and the superior train stops at the meet unless the switch is lined and the track clear.

    Trains of the same class going the same way may pass each other. Sections of one schedule may pass only after swapping orders, signals and numbers. A train that catches a disabled train may pass it and, if needed, take over its schedule and orders.

    Protecting the train

    When a train might be overtaken, the flagman protects the rear (Rule 99). Moving: drop lighted fusees at intervals. Stopped: walk back far enough, lay two torpedoes, add fusees when needed; when called in, leave the torpedoes and a lit fusee behind. The engineman whistles the flagman out and recalls him. The front is protected the same way when needed. Conductors are also responsible for every switch their crew uses: line it back, and never leave it open for another train unless one of that train's crew is tending it.

    Where block signals govern trains in the same direction, the signals take over from superiority (Rule 251).

    3. Train orders

    A train order is how the dispatcher changes the timetable: creating extras, setting meets, giving right, making trains late. An order stays in force until it is fulfilled, superseded or annulled.

    Getting an order to a crew

    1. Orders are numbered in sequence, starting again each day at midnight.
    2. The dispatcher calls each office with a "31" or "19" signal, the direction and how many copies to make.
    3. Place names and numbers are said and then spelled; zero is "naught".
    4. The operator writes up Clearance Form A, listing how many orders the train has and their numbers, with no erasures, and reads it back. The dispatcher answers "OK" with the time and his initials.
    5. The crew (conductor, engineman, fireman) checks that the clearance matches the orders in hand.

    If the wire fails after all the orders are complete but before the OK, the operator may still deliver them, marking the clearance "Wire has failed". At an open office the train order signal shows stop, and no train passes it without a clearance.

    How trains are named in orders

    Regular trains by number (No 7), sections by position (Second 7), extras by engine number and direction (Extra 4410 West), work extras as Work Extra and the engine number. A foreign railroad's engine gets its initials in front.

    4. Yard limits

    Yard limits are a stretch of main track, set by mileposts in the timetable and marked by signs, where engines can switch and shuffle without asking the dispatcher each time. Inside them, trains and engines don't protect against each other, so everyone moves slowly enough to stop for whatever is ahead.

    • Standard Code Rule 93: you may use the main track but must still clear first-class trains. You don't need protection against other trains; move at yard speed unless you know the main is clear.
    • GCOR 6.13: restricted speed unless a block signal shows better than Approach. Engines get out of the way of trains and never delay passenger trains. In CTC the control operator still authorizes main track moves.
    • Inside yard limits the yardmaster or agent is in charge if one is on duty; otherwise, usually the conductor or foreman of the first crew there.

    5. Track warrants

    A track warrant is a fill-in form the dispatcher reads over the radio. It is the modern replacement for train orders on lines without signals.

    The exchange

    1. The crew asks for a warrant, saying what they'll do, on which tracks and for how long.
    2. The dispatcher reads it at copying speed. The crew writes it down and reads every word back, not just the box numbers.
    3. The dispatcher gives an OK time and initials. Until the OK time is on the form, the warrant is just paper.
    4. Conductor and engineer each have a dated copy.
    5. The warrant lasts until the crew releases it. To report clear, the whole train must be off the limits, and switches it used must be back to normal (inside yard limits this last part doesn't apply).

    Where the limits start and end

    When a station is named as a limit, the crew's authority covers the siding switches that matter for a meet: from the far switch of the first station and to the near switch of the last station. The guide's memory aid is "first is last and last is first". With no siding, the station sign is the limit. "Hold main track at last named point" carries the limit on past the near switch, up to but not including the far switch, so the train can hold the main while an opposing train uses the siding. A train must never foul a switch at either end that an opposing train could use to clear.

    The form's 17 boxes, by purpose

    • Cancelling: 1 voids an earlier warrant.
    • Authority: 2 and 3 "Proceed from … to" (one direction; backing up needs separate permission). 4 "Work between" (both directions, for locals and work trains).
    • Timing: 5 not in effect until a time, 6 expires at a time, 7 not in effect until another train arrives.
    • At the far end: 8 hold main track, 9 don't foul limits ahead of another train, 10 clear the main track.
    • Speed: 11 restricted speed because a train is in the limits, 12 because men or equipment are, 13 and 14 mph limits.
    • Other: 15 no flag needed against following trains, 16 track bulletins, 17 anything else (such as "joint with" another train).

    Warrants govern only the main track. In sidings, move at a speed that lets you stop in half the distance you can see. Outside yard limits, nobody goes on the main without one. The TWC guide says model railroads can drop boxes 5, 6 and 12 to 15.

    6. Signals and whistles

    Reading a signal

    The aspect is what you see; the indication is what it tells you to do; the name is the railroad's label for it. Under route signaling (most model railroads) the signal tells you which way you're lined and how clear the blocks ahead are; speeds come from the timetable. Under speed signaling the signal tells you how fast to go.

    • Green (Clear): this block and the next block are not occupied. Yellow (Approach): this block is clear but the next block is occupied.
    • On a two-head mast, red over green or red over yellow is the same idea for the diverging route. It is not a stop: "it's not red unless it's all red".
    • What a stop means depends on the plate. A or no plate: absolute; wait for permission. Number plate: stop, then go on at restricted speed. G: may pass at restricted speed without stopping, to avoid stalling on a grade. SA: an interlocking signal; ask the operator. D: distant signal warning of an interlocking ahead. P: a detector in the block that the crew must check.

    ABS divides track into automatic blocks. APB adds a rule for opposing moves: once a train enters between sidings, opposing signals all go red to the next siding. CTC lets a dispatcher set switches and signals at control points. An interlocking makes conflicting routes impossible, run from a tower.

    Hand signals (Standard Code)

    The motion gives the meaning: a swing across the track stops you; up-and-down proceeds; held out level means slow; a full circle means back up.

    7. Semaphores

    A semaphore shows its aspect by the angle of a blade by day and colored lights at night. Horizontal is always stop; treat it as a closed gate.

    • Upper quadrant blades rise to clear (positions about 0, 45 and 90 degrees); they spread from around 1900. Lower quadrant blades drop (0, 30, 60).
    • Lower quadrant isn't fail-safe: if the linkage breaks, gravity pulls the blade toward clear.
    • Parts: blade, roundels (the colored lenses), the spectacle that holds them, lamp, mechanism, housing.
    • Semaphores served both as train order boards and as block signals; some were fixed at 45 degrees as a permanent warning.

    On a model railroad, ask the host what a semaphore means.

    8. Crew duties and speed

    • Conductor: in charge of the train and its paperwork. The crew follows the conductor unless that would be unsafe or against the rules. When two crews combine, the senior conductor takes charge. The conductor warns the engineer of restrictions and authority limits ahead, at least two miles before.
    • Engineer: runs the engine safely and asks the conductor about cars needing special handling. With no conductor present, the crew follows the engineer.
    • Everyone in the cab calls out each signal by name and keeps watching it. If they disagree, take the most restrictive reading. Anyone may stop the train if the rules aren't being followed.
    • Yardmaster: runs the yard; where one is on duty, crews take the yardmaster's instructions. Dispatcher: supervises train movement; operators work under the dispatcher.

    Speed words

    Restricted speed means you can stop short of anything in your path: a train, a car, workers, a red signal, a misaligned switch or derail. The GCOR puts it as within half your range of vision, never over 20 mph, watching for broken rail, until the front of the train is past the restriction. The Standard Code says the same without the numbers. Off the main track (and outside signal systems) the same half-range rule applies.

    A burning fusee by the track means stop, then restricted speed for a mile past it, unless it is beyond the first rail of the next track over. Moving against the current of traffic needs a warrant or bulletin, except in places such as yard limits that have their own rule.

    Where the guides disagree

    These cards follow the 1949 Standard Code (Study Guide §1.0), which the TT&TO primer names as the reference document. Where a question depends on one of these, check which rulebook it refers to.

    • Clearing time (S-87, S-89). The Standard Code says extras clear opposing regular trains by not less than 5 minutes. The TT&TO primer quotes the rules with 10 minutes and works its Rule 87 example with 10. Its own appendix example then uses 5.
    • Yard limits speed (Rule 93). Standard Code: yard speed unless the main track is known to be clear. TT&TO primer: a speed that permits stopping within one-half the range of vision. Yard Limits guide: restricted speed unless block signal protection is present. GCOR 6.13: restricted speed unless a block signal shows something better than Approach.
    • Restricted speed. Standard Code gives no figure. GCOR 6.27 adds “stop within half the range of vision” and “not exceed 20 MPH”.
    • Whistle signals. Standard Code 14(a) one short = apply brakes, stop. GCOR (2) one long = when stopped, air brakes applied, pressure equalized. Three shorts (back up) and the crossing signal — — o — match in both.
