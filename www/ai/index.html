The Diverging Route — AI in Model Railroading

Model railroading is many hobbies in one. A single layout asks you to be a historian and a carpenter, an electrician and a signal engineer, a programmer and a photographer, and in the end a railroader running trains with friends. On a real railroad, the diverging route is the other way through a switch, where a train leaves the straight main line and heads somewhere new. The Diverging Route takes that turn: it brings AI into each of those crafts, often in ways the hobby hasn’t tried before.

The main line is still there. Wiring every turnout by hand and learning every trick of the craft is as rewarding as it ever was. What Artificial Intelligence adds is help with the skills we haven’t had time to learn: reading a wiring diagram, writing a script, drawing a part for the 3D printer, digging a fact out of an old record. Jobs that could stop a project for months can take an afternoon. That leaves more time for building, running trains and sharing the layout with friends. The Diverging Route is different, but the destination is the same.

Getting started

New to AI? You don’t need to understand how it works to use it well. These habits made the biggest difference, roughly in the order to try them, and the topics above show them at work: Prototype research keeps a source with every picture, and The workshop keeps decisions written down in one place.

  • Start with something you already knowAsk about a locomotive, a prototype railroad or a technique you know well. You will quickly see where it is sharp and where it bluffs, and that tells you how far to trust it on things you don’t know.
  • Give it the whole pictureSay what you are modelling, the era, the scale and what you already have, then say what you want back: a list, a table, a plan, a paragraph. Good context beats clever wording.
  • Make it show its sourcesAsk where a fact came from, then look. It can sound completely sure and still be wrong about a date, a road number or a part number. Check anything that matters against the real thing: a photograph, a manual, a tape measure, a test run.
  • Work in small stepsAsk for one thing, look at it, then ask for the next. Let it suggest and you decide. Small steps are easy to check and easy to undo.
  • Hand it the tedious partsLists, paperwork, renaming files, tidying data, first drafts and small scripts are where it saves the most time. Keep the fun parts, like building and running trains, for yourself.
  • Write down what you decideIt does not remember earlier chats. Keep a short note of your choices and the reasons, and paste it in when you start again.
  • Keep a master copySave your own files before it changes anything, and keep working versions apart from finished ones. If something goes wrong, you can go back.
  • Keep the creditIf it helps you find history, photographs or quotes, keep the source with them. A picture or a fact with no source is hard to trust later.

Good first things to try

  • Ask it to explain something you have never quite understood, such as how a passing siding works, and then to quiz you on it.
  • Paste in a long article or forum thread and ask for the five points that matter.
  • Describe a switch list or waybill you would like and ask for a layout you can print.
  • Paste an error message from your layout software and ask what it means and what to try.
  • Give it a photograph and ask for a caption, then correct the details it gets wrong.
  • Ask it to compare two ways of building something, with the good and bad of each.

Going further

Once chat feels comfortable, tools such as Claude Code, Cursor and Codex can work directly on a folder of your own files: your layout’s JMRI profile, your scripts, your documents. They read what is there, make changes you can review, and run checks. The key is a short instructions file at the top of the folder, usually called AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md, that says what the project is, how it is organised, what must never be touched and how to check the work. Write it once and every session starts with the same understanding instead of from scratch. Keep a backup or put the folder under version control such as Git, so every change can be undone, and have the agent propose changes for you to approve. For how to write that file, see the AGENTS.md guide, which Codex and Cursor read, and Anthropic’s best practices for Claude Code.
