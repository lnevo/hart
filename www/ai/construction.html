Benchwork is where a wrong number costs real wood. The pattern that worked here was the same each time: work the design out in words, turn it into a script so every drawing comes from the same numbers, and then measure the real thing and let the measurement win.

The job

A helix climbs from staging to the main level. It has to keep a gentle grade, leave room for trains to pass underneath each turn, fit the room, and be buildable from sheets of plywood by one person.

What AI did

Radius, grade, clearance, how the roadbed would be cut and joined, and the order it would all go up were worked through in conversation before any wood was cut. The alternatives that were turned down were written down with the reasons.

What came out

A 13-page construction plan with frames, braces, bracket heights, roadbed cut sheets and the build sequence, and a design record with the formulas behind it.

Verdict
  • Worked well: some of the most valuable AI work on the whole project. The plan held through the build; the corrections that came later were about real materials, not the design.
  • Needed me: the decisions. The record keeps the options that were turned down, and choosing among them was mine.

02Printed fixtures for the helix

Tool
Claude
When
September to October 2026
Status
Done; helix built and tracked

The job

Cutting dozens of identical half-lap joints and setting every bracket at the right height by hand invites drift. Jigs would make each cut the same.

What AI did

Claude turned the plan into scripts, and the scripts into 3D-printable files: router templates for the half-laps and bracket pockets, height spacers, shims and other fixtures, 47 files in all. Each was checked in software as a single watertight body before printing.

Then the real wood had its say. The plywood measured 0.72 inch, not the nominal 3/4, so every height was recalculated from that. The first height template was settled by test-fit at 1-1/32 inch, under the calculated figure. One batch of spacers printed 1.65 mm short of the gap they had to fill, so a measured correction went in. All of that lives in one build-status document, which says plainly that where it disagrees with the original plan, it wins.

What came out

A finished, tracked helix in about a month, and the first time AI's work was something I could pick up and build with. None of the 47 parts is a model of anything. They are tools, and they went on the bench and into the build.

Verdict
  • Worked well: going from a plan to physical tools quickly, and changing every part at once when a number changed.
  • Needed me: calipers and test prints. Software checks prove a part is printable, not that it fits.

03Framing drawings and cut plans

Tool
Claude
When
October 2026
Status
Drawn; main level resumes after Thanksgiving

The job

The main level sits 15 inches above staging. It needed framing that stays shallow enough to reach the staging tracks underneath, plywood cut from as few sheets as possible, and a shopping list.

What AI did

The level was described once, as geometry in a script, and the drawings are generated from it: a 7-page framing set, plywood cut sheets for four 3/4 inch and five 1/2 inch sheets with trammel radii, 11 pages of marking sheets that give every point to mark from the factory edges, and a lumber cut list that uses the sticks already on hand first. A bill of materials was priced from the local supplier's listings. The staging level has a framing drawing made the same way.

What came out

A complete paper kit for the main level. When a joist or a closet box moved, the drawings, cut plan and cut list all moved together.

Verdict
  • Worked well: one source for every drawing, so they cannot disagree, and marking sheets I could not have made by hand in any sensible time.
  • Still to prove: the cut plan meets the saw after Thanksgiving.

04Station maps in plastic

Tool
Claude
When
November 2026
Status
In progress

The job

The paper station maps show a crew where each switch is. As fascia plates that hold the toggle switches themselves, with the track and lettering in a second colour, the map and the switch become one object.

What AI did

It did not start with the big map. A test panel 2 inches square with one toggle came first, to answer three plain questions: how big the pocket must be, whether small crush ribs hold the toggle without glue, and whether a finger can still work it through a 1 mm skin. The real toggles were measured with calipers, and they turned out smaller than their own drawing. With those answers the Barn map was modelled: measured pockets, the thin skin, the ribs, and channels at the back that gather four toggles' wires to one exit. It follows the same drawing as the paper map.

What came out

A proven test panel and a modelled Barn map. Barn has not printed successfully yet: the lettering on the first layer will not stick to the plate.

Verdict
  • Worked well: a small test before the big part, so the questions were answered for the price of a coaster.
  • Needed me: the printer. Adhesion is a matter of the plate and the slicer, and that is where it stands.
